IRS Letter 566: your return is under audit by mail

Letter 566 is the letter the IRS uses to start an audit by mail (a correspondence examination). It tells you your return was selected and lists the documents to send. You must respond by the date on the letter, and you can ordinarily get a one-time 30-day extension.

Updated: September 29, 2026 · Based on information from IRS.gov · General information, not tax advice.

What it isThe initial contact letter for an audit by mail: it tells you which items on your return the IRS is reviewing and what information it needs.
DeadlineThe date shown on the letter. You can ordinarily get a one-time automatic 30-day extension.
If you don't respondThe IRS completes the audit without your information, disallows the items identified and sends a report with its proposed changes.
How it startsThe IRS notifies you of an audit by mail. It doesn't start audits by telephone.

What does Letter 566 mean?

An audit by mail starts with a letter saying you've been selected and listing the information you need to send. According to the IRS, a return can be selected at random, by computer comparison with similar returns, or because it's related to another audit. A refund is not necessarily a trigger for an audit.

Don't confuse it with notice CP566, which is a different IRS notice.

How to respond, step by step

  1. Confirm the letter is real. Look up the letter number on IRS.gov or call the IRS at 800-829-1040. If the letter lists 866-897-0177 or 866-897-0161 as the contact number, you can also check the audit status in your IRS online account.
  2. Read which items are being reviewed. The letter says which parts of your return the IRS is examining and which documents it needs.
  3. Gather copies, not originals. Send photocopies of receipts, statements and other records. If you fax, put your name and Social Security number on every page.
  4. If you need more time, ask in writing to the fax number on the letter (or by mail if you can't fax). The IRS can ordinarily grant a one-time automatic 30-day extension.
  5. Send your response by the date on the letter, by mail or to the fax number shown.
  6. Review the IRS report. If you agree with the proposed changes, sign the agreement page. If you disagree, don't sign it: send documents and an explanation.

What if I disagree with the result?

The IRS describes several options:

  • An informal conference with the examiner's manager.
  • A conference with the IRS Independent Office of Appeals (Form 12203 if the amount in dispute is $25,000 or less).
  • Mediation.
  • A petition to the U.S. Tax Court after you receive a statutory notice of deficiency, such as CP3219A.
